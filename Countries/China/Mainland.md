# Unschooling in Mainland China

## Compulsory education

Mainland China provides for nine years of compulsory education. Compulsory schooling ordinarily begins at the age of six, although entry may be deferred until the age of seven in areas where the necessary conditions for enrolment are not available. The compulsory period therefore generally corresponds approximately to ages 6–15.

Article 4 of the *Compulsory Education Law of the People’s Republic of China* provides that:

> All school-age children and adolescents who have the nationality of the People’s Republic of China shall, in accordance with law, enjoy the equal right, and fulfil the obligation, to receive compulsory education, regardless of sex, ethnic status or race, family financial conditions, religious belief, etc.

The expression sometimes translated as “nationality” within the list of prohibited distinctions is *minzu* (民族), which refers to ethnicity or ethnic group. By contrast, the preceding phrase *having the nationality of the People’s Republic of China* (具有中华人民共和国国籍) expressly defines the provision by reference to PRC nationality.

## Home education

Chinese law does not recognize home education as a general substitute for school attendance for children who are subject to compulsory education.

Article 5 of the *Compulsory Education Law* requires parents or other statutory guardians to ensure that school-age children enrol in school and receive and complete compulsory education. Under Article 58, guardians who, without a valid justification, fail to send a child to school may be subject to administrative intervention and may be ordered to rectify the situation.

The Ministry of Education clarified this position in its 2019 *Provisions Prohibiting Obstruction of the Implementation of Compulsory Education*. The notice identifies, among conduct obstructing compulsory education, arrangements whereby parents or guardians keep compulsory-school-age children at home to study instead of enrolling them in accordance with the law, and directs local education authorities to intervene in such cases.

Accordingly, for PRC-national children subject to compulsory education, homeschooling or unschooling is not recognized as a lawful general alternative to attendance at a school providing compulsory education.

## Foreign nationals

Article 4 of the *Compulsory Education Law* applies expressly to school-age children and adolescents who possess PRC nationality. Its compulsory-education obligation therefore does not, on its terms, extend to children who are solely foreign nationals. UNESCO's China education profile similarly states that the relevant compulsory-education provisions do not apply to non-citizen children holding foreign passports.

Separate regulatory provisions govern schools established for the children of foreign nationals lawfully residing in China. Such institutions may provide preschool, primary and secondary education and may operate according to foreign educational models.

The legal position of foreign-national children is therefore distinct from that of PRC nationals. Their exclusion from the compulsory-education obligation in Article 4 does not, however, amount to the creation of a statutory homeschooling regime or to formal state recognition of home education.
