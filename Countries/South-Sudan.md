# Unschooling in South Sudan

## Compulsory education

Article 29(2) of the Transitional Constitution requires all levels of
government to provide free and compulsory primary education. The General
Education Act, 2012 defines primary education as an eight-year course from
Primary 1 to Primary 8. The Ministry's National General Education Policy
describes primary education as the basic cycle for children aged 5–14 and
directs that it be free and compulsory through Primary 8.

Section 18 of the Act requires every parent or guardian to ensure that a child
is enrolled in and attends school on reaching age 5 or 6. The 2017 policy
clarifies the intended starting age as 5 in urban settings and 6 in rural
settings. A County Education Department must investigate a failure to enrol;
schools must similarly investigate non-attendance after enrolment. A parent or
guardian who neglects or refuses to enrol and send a child to school may be
fined.

The Child Act, 2008 independently describes primary education as free and
compulsory. It also makes parents responsible for ensuring that their children
receive full-time education suitable to their age, ability and aptitude, and
links provision for special educational needs to regular school attendance.

## Home education and unschooling

South Sudanese law contains no general framework for home education. The
applicable statutory duty is enrolment and school attendance. Under section
18(6) of the General Education Act, a State Ministry may exempt a learner from
compulsory *formal* school attendance **only on medical grounds**. The Act
does not provide an exemption based on parental preference, pedagogical
approach, religion, distance, or the family's capacity to educate at home.

The reference to “Alternative Education” in the Act must be understood within
its statutory definition: a *prescribed course of study* for children or adults
who missed formal education, allowing a return to the formal system or the
acquisition of skills through alternative learning or home-study. The Ministry's
Alternative Education System (AES) is an official, organised non-formal system
for disadvantaged and out-of-school learners. Its documented programmes include
accelerated learning, community girls' schools, pastoralist education, adult
literacy, intensive English courses and interactive radio instruction. These
programmes are directed to defined learner groups, use prescribed content, and
provide pathways to formal education; they do not constitute a general
entitlement to parent-directed education at home.

The National General Education Policy supports this interpretation. It requires
school-age learners to attend lessons regularly, characterises attendance as
compulsory, and subjects AES centres or schools to Ministry inspection. The
Policy further provides that the national curriculum applies to all learning
institutions other than international schools.

## Citizenship and residency

The constitutional right to education is phrased as a right of citizens. The
parental enrolment and attendance duty in section 18 of the General Education
Act is not, however, textually confined to citizens. The 2017 policy also
contemplates access to primary education for refugee children. No separate
home-education rule for foreign children has been identified.
