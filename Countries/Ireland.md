# Unschooling in Ireland

## Compulsory education

In Ireland, compulsory education generally begins at the age of 6. For the purposes of the compulsory-education provisions of the *Education (Welfare) Act 2000*, a child is a person resident in the State who has reached the age of 6 and who has not reached the age of 16 or completed three years of post-primary education, whichever occurs later. The statutory definition does not extend beyond the age of 18.

The Constitution expressly recognises the freedom of parents to provide education outside recognised schools. Article 42 provides that parents may educate their children “in their homes or in private schools or in schools recognised or established by the State”, while permitting the State to require that children receive a “certain minimum education, moral, intellectual and social”.

## Home education

Parents who elect to educate a child at home must apply to Tusla's Alternative Education Assessment and Registration Service (AEARS) for the child to be entered on the register maintained under section 14 of the *Education (Welfare) Act 2000*. Registration is subject to an assessment of whether the education being provided satisfies the statutory requirement of a certain minimum education.

Under the current procedure, home education may commence once AEARS has confirmed in writing that it has received a complete and valid application; a parent is therefore not required to await completion of the assessment or a final registration decision. Until such confirmation has been received, the child should remain in a recognised school where applicable.

Home-educated children are not required to follow the national curriculum. Tusla states that parents may adopt a range of educational approaches, resources and supports, provided that the child receives a certain minimum education appropriate to the child's age, aptitude and ability.

The official assessment guidelines recognise that home education may differ substantially from conventional schooling. They contemplate informal or semi-formal education, learning arising from everyday experiences, flexible and child-centred approaches, and a greater degree of self-directed learning than would ordinarily occur in school.

An interest-driven approach is therefore compatible with the Irish legal framework, although it remains subject to the statutory minimum-education standard. The education provided must support appropriate development and progress and should address, among other matters, oral language, literacy and numeracy, the child's broader learning needs and abilities, a range of learning areas, and sufficiently frequent and regular opportunities for learning. Some degree of planning is expected, although the guidelines recognise that planning in a home-education setting may be substantially less formal than in a school environment.

Under section 14, the initial assessment considers the education being provided or proposed, the materials being used, and the time devoted to education. Where this is insufficient to determine whether the statutory minimum is satisfied, a more comprehensive assessment may include observation of the child, inspection of the premises and educational materials, and consideration of the child's intellectual, emotional and physical development.

## Irish language

The Irish language forms part of the assessment framework, but the official guidance does not establish an inflexible requirement that every home-educated child follow the Irish-language curriculum applicable in recognised schools.

Following the Supreme Court's decision in *DPP v Best*, the guidelines provide that, where a child's first language is English, the availability of opportunities to learn Irish should be considered as part of the overall assessment, having regard to the child's abilities, aptitudes, needs, personality and interests. Where the child's first language is neither Irish nor English, the assessment should instead consider opportunities to acquire the child's mother tongue together with English or Irish, according to the child's individual circumstances.

Accordingly, the provision of opportunities to learn Irish is **a factor relevant to the assessment of whether a certain minimum education is being provided, rather than an inflexible subject requirement equivalent to that applicable in recognised schools**.

## Foreign nationals

The compulsory-education provisions of the *Education (Welfare) Act 2000* are framed by reference to residence rather than nationality: the statutory definition of a child refers to a person “resident in the State”. Children of foreign nationals who are resident in Ireland are therefore subject to the same registration and minimum-education requirements as other resident children.

The assessment guidelines expressly contemplate children whose first language is neither English nor Irish and provide for their linguistic and cultural circumstances to be taken into account in the assessment process.

## Statistics

| Year | Children on the home-education register at year end |
| ---- | --------------------------------------------------: |
| 2023 | 2,447 |
| 2024 | 2,325 |
| 2025 | 2,592 |

The reduction recorded in 2024 partly reflects an administrative change rather than a corresponding decline in the number of children receiving home education. In August 2024, Tusla removed 475 young people aged 16 or over from the section 14 register following the introduction of revised criteria governing the cessation of registrations.

Tusla's 2025 Annual Report records **2,592 children on the home-education register at the end of 2025**.
