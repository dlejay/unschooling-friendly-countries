# Unschooling in North Korea

## Compulsory education

North Korea has a system of twelve years of free compulsory education, running
approximately from age 5 to 17:

* one compulsory year of preschool;
* five years of primary school;
* three years of lower secondary school; and
* three years of higher secondary school.

The education system is operated by the state. North Korean law requires local
People’s Committees and the relevant authorities to identify children who have
reached school age each year and ensure that they attend an educational
institution.

“Free” describes the system’s formal legal status and should not be understood
as evidence that families never bear education-related costs in practice.

## Unschooling

North Korean law does not provide a recognised route for elective home
education.

Available descriptions of the Education Law and the Law on General Education
indicate that school-age children must be included in educational institutions
and caused to attend school. As general education is provided through
state-operated institutions, homeschooling—and therefore unschooling—is not a
lawful alternative to school attendance.

Children with disabilities may attend specialised schools or, depending on
their condition, delay attendance. This is not a general entitlement to
education at home.

## Foreign nationals

The available public legal sources do not establish a general exemption from
compulsory education, or a right to homeschool, for foreign children living in
North Korea.

A school operated by the Russian embassy is reported to exist, but an embassy
school is not equivalent to a legal home-education regime. The claim that the
school-attendance requirements “do not apply to foreigners” should therefore
not be made without a specific legal source.

## Private tutoring

Private tutoring should be distinguished from homeschooling: children receiving
such lessons normally remain enrolled in state schools, while tutors provide
additional instruction privately and for payment.

In May 2024, Daily NK reported that a woman in Chongjin had been sentenced to
six months in a disciplinary labour camp after being caught teaching
schoolchildren at her home.

In October 2025, Daily NK reported a further crackdown in Chongjin. According
to the outlet, several private tutors had been sent to labour camps, including
one tutor given a six-month hard-labour sentence. The report said that accepting
payment for lessons at home was being treated as an illegal “non-socialist”
activity.

These reports rely on anonymous sources inside North Korea and cannot be
independently verified, but they provide evidence that privately organised
education outside the state system is actively suppressed.
