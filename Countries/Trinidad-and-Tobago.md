# Unschooling in Trinidad and Tobago

## Constitutional and statutory framework

Section 4(f) of the Constitution recognises the right of a parent or guardian
to provide a school of their own choice for the education of a child. The
operative rules for compulsory attendance and home instruction are contained
in Part III of the Education Act, Chap. 39:01.

Under section 76 of the Act, compulsory school age begins at 5 and ends when a
child reaches 16. Section 77 requires the parent of a child within that age
range to secure efficient full-time education, suitable to the child's age,
ability and aptitude, through regular attendance at school.

Section 78(a) creates an exception where, in the Minister's opinion, the child
is receiving satisfactory instruction at home or elsewhere. Home instruction
therefore operates as a ministerial exemption from school attendance rather
than as an alternative that a parent may adopt without prior administrative
action.

Sections 83 and 84 provide for summary proceedings against a parent who fails
to secure attendance where the child is not legally excused.

## Home education procedure

The Ministry of Education's *Policy on Home Schooling*, dated January 2023,
establishes the procedure for obtaining that exemption. A separate application
must be submitted for each child to the Office of the Chief Education Officer,
together with the child's birth certificate and proof of the parent's
identity. The application must describe the proposed educational approach,
the curriculum and resources, the qualifications of the parent and any tutor,
the means of recording progress, the educational goals and a timetable
identifying subjects, days and teaching times.

The application form states that applications ordinarily take four to six
weeks. A child aged 5 to 16 must remain enrolled at, and attending, a
registered school while the application is being considered. If approval is
refused, the Ministry is to facilitate placement in a public school; the
parent may instead enrol the child in a registered private school and may
reapply for home education in the following term or school year.

Permission is granted for a stated period. Parents must apply again each year,
and continued permission depends on the Ministry's assessment of an end-of-year
report prepared by the tutor and signed by the parent or guardian.

## Curriculum and supervision

The policy does not make use of the Ministry's curriculum mandatory in every
case. An applicant may propose a different curriculum, although the policy
recommends the national curriculum for preparation for examinations and later
entry into local schools or tertiary institutions.

This flexibility remains subject to a structured programme and official
supervision. The parent must permit assessments arranged by the Ministry to
determine the child's proficiency or progress against Ministry school
standards or an established individualised pathway. Ministry officers may
conduct virtual or in-person visits for safeguarding purposes and, where
progress has led to probation, to observe instruction. The Ministry may
require an adapted curriculum, place the child on probation for one term and,
if improvement does not follow, deem the instruction inadequate and recommend
placement in the public-school system.

The policy's definition acknowledges that home education may include
experiential learning based on life experiences. It nevertheless defines the
practice in terms of structured learning activities, and the prescribed
application requires a curriculum, subjects and a teaching timetable. These
requirements also apply where an applicant proposes a curriculum other than
the Ministry's curriculum.

## Foreign families

The policy states that it applies to children of compulsory school age who are
nationals of Trinidad and Tobago. For a child who is not a citizen by birth,
the accompanying application form requires proof of Trinidad and Tobago
citizenship or permanent residence. Neither document identifies eligibility
based solely on temporary immigration status.
